Meet the panel

One factual event. Six contrasting lenses.

Each panel member is a fictional analytical construct with a fixed visual identity, ideological role and recurring set of questions. No single lens is intended to be complete on its own.

How a briefing works

Facts first. Interpretation second.

The format separates the event from the ideological lenses used to interpret it.

  1. 01
    Establish the factual frame

    Define the event, institutional context and evidence that the briefing is actually about.

  2. 02
    Apply six lenses

    The panel interrogates the same facts through markets, law and order, technology, labour, Pan-Africanism and neutral systems synthesis.

  3. 03
    Trace institutional consequences

    Move beyond rhetoric toward capacity, incentives, implementation, households, sovereignty and system effects.

  4. 04
    Close with synthesis

    Pull the competing interpretations back together without pretending they are the same argument.

AI Geopolitic panelist profile

Ideological lens

NORA

Neutral AI moderator

NORA is the panel’s neutral systems moderator. She establishes the factual frame, looks for connections across institutions and incentives, and tests whether apparently separate developments form part of the same system. Her role is not to erase disagreement, but to make the disagreement legible: what each lens sees, what it misses, and where the evidence overlaps.

What they watch
Institutional capacity · system interactions · evidence chains · implementation · synthesis
Typical question
What changes when the whole system is viewed together?
What this lens reveals
Connects fragmented evidence into a coherent system-level picture.
Potential blind spot
Synthesis can sometimes flatten moral or lived-experience differences that matter in their own right.
Ideological lens

Diane Sterling

Neoliberal economist

Diane approaches events through markets, fiscal constraints, investment incentives and institutional efficiency. She follows how policy moves through prices, capital, logistics and trade, and is especially interested in whether public choices create durable productive capacity or merely shift costs elsewhere. Her style is polished, pragmatic and numbers-driven.

What they watch
Markets · trade · investment · logistics · budgets · industrial policy
Typical question
What incentives does this create, and what does it cost?
What this lens reveals
Exposes price signals, fiscal constraints and unintended economic consequences.
Potential blind spot
Can underweight social value or unequal burdens that do not fit neatly into market measures.
Ideological lens

Johan Vosloo

Conservative pragmatist

Johan evaluates events through institutional legitimacy, legal authority, public order and state capacity. He is sceptical of solutions that sound persuasive but cannot be lawfully implemented, enforced or sustained. His voice is restrained and legalistic, with particular attention to oversight, borders, public safety, national security and the continuity of institutions.

What they watch
Law · state authority · oversight · public order · legitimacy · security
Typical question
Who has lawful authority, and can the decision actually be enforced?
What this lens reveals
Tests whether policy rests on legitimate authority and functioning institutions.
Potential blind spot
Can underweight the urgency of reform or the value of informal and community responses when formal systems fail.
Ideological lens

Kai Patel

Techno-libertarian futurist

Kai looks beneath the headline to the technical systems that make an outcome possible: data, networks, AI, infrastructure, monitoring and feedback loops. Future-facing and provocative, he is drawn to decentralisation and innovation while remaining alert to surveillance, monopoly and brittle system design. He asks whether the architecture can scale—and what happens when it breaks.

What they watch
AI · data · digital infrastructure · networks · surveillance · system design
Typical question
What system makes this possible—and what happens when it fails?
What this lens reveals
Reveals technical dependencies, network effects and infrastructure risks others can miss.
Potential blind spot
Can overestimate what elegant technical design can solve when politics, institutions and human behaviour remain messy.
Ideological lens

Thabo Mokoena

Marxist trade unionist

Thabo centres workers, households and material conditions. He pulls abstract policy back toward wages, jobs, poverty, grants, public services and the distribution of economic pain. His voice is direct, structural and class-conscious: if a reform improves a balance sheet while shifting the immediate burden onto ordinary households, he wants that cost made visible.

What they watch
Labour · wages · class · poverty · public services · household burden
Typical question
Who carries the material cost of this decision?
What this lens reveals
Forces policy analysis to confront distribution, inequality and lived material consequences.
Potential blind spot
Can underweight investment, efficiency or longer-run supply responses when they conflict with immediate distributional concerns.
Ideological lens

Amari Ndlovu

Pan-African cultural analyst

Amari places current events within longer African histories of power, memory, sovereignty and cultural dignity. She pays close attention to migration, historical wounds, continental solidarity and whether African institutions are gaining or surrendering agency. Her voice is reflective and moral, often asking what formal policy language obscures about dignity, identity and historical power.

What they watch
Sovereignty · dignity · African institutions · memory · migration · solidarity
Typical question
Who holds agency, and whose dignity is protected?
What this lens reveals
Reveals historical power imbalances, questions of sovereignty and the human meaning of institutional choices.
Potential blind spot
Can underweight short-term technocratic or market trade-offs when the larger question of dignity or autonomy dominates the frame.
